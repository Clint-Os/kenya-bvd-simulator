# Modelling Bundibugyo spread across two Kenyan cities

The published literature supports a defensible modelling article on Bundibugyo virus disease (BVD) spread in Nairobi and Mombasa, but the evidence base is **inverted relative to planning need**: transmissibility is characterised by three mutually corroborating 2026 estimates clustering around **R ≈ 1.95–2.51 for early uncontrolled spread**, while the single parameter that most directly governs isolation-bed demand — length of hospital stay — has **no usable published distribution for Bundibugyo at all**. Two bodies of theory are mature and directly transferable: delayed-feedback epidemic models produce **Hopf bifurcations at a critical response delay τ₀** derived from a standard characteristic equation P(λ)+Q(λ)e^{−λτ}=0, and limited-treatment-capacity models since Wang & Ruan (2004) produce **backward bifurcations and bistability below a critical bed number b_c**, so disease can persist when R₀<1 — yet neither framework has ever been applied to Ebola, which is where this article's novelty lies. The intervention evidence (all drawn from Zaire ebolavirus in West Africa 2014–16, DRC 2018–20, and Sudan virus in Uganda 2022, since **no Bundibugyo-specific effect-size study exists**) is quantitatively rich and consistent: isolating roughly **70% of cases ends an epidemic**, each **30-day delay in bed scale-up roughly triples the peak**, and **unsafe burials generate ~2.58 secondary cases each**. Kenya's own 2026 outbreak — **2 confirmed cases, 1 death, imported via JKIA on 3 October** — exposes the supply-side asymmetry this article should centre on: of the **~241 designated isolation beds, ~78% sit in Nairobi** (130 in one private hospital alone) and **Mombasa has only 4**, despite being a port city of 1.2 million with transit links straight into the DRC. Two citations in the modelling brief must be corrected before they enter the article, and the two most parameter-dense sources are not peer-reviewed; both points are developed below.

## Bundibugyo transmissibility is well characterised, but length-of-stay is a published void

The clearest finding for the Kenyan planning question is that **BVD transmissibility is better documented than almost any other parameter**, and it tells a consistent two-phase story. The 2026 DRC/Uganda outbreak yields three 2026-relevant reproduction-number estimates that agree on an early-phase R near 2 falling below 1 by mid-2026 in Ituri. A phylodynamic R(t) analysis of genomes collected to 6 September 2026 estimates **R = 1.95 (95% HPD 1.59–2.32)** before outbreak declaration (1 Mar–15 May), **1.17 (0.87–1.50)** through mid-June, and **0.72 (0.59–0.86)** from mid-June to late August, with a P1 growth rate of **0.049/day (doubling time ~14 days)** ([Virological.org, 1 Oct 2026](https://virological.org/t/reproduction-number-of-the-2026-bundibugyo-virus-disease-outbreak-over-time-phylodynamic-estimates-from-genomes-collected-up-to-6-september-2026/1047)). The CDC's branching-process scenario model gives a median **R₀ = 2.51 (IQI 2.27–2.82)** ([MMWR 2026;75:285–289, via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13257927/)), and the epiforecasts BVDOutbreakSize model infers a doubling time of **11.7 days (95% HPD 6.8–17.5)** ([epiforecasts methods](https://epiforecasts.io/BVDOutbreakSize/stable/methods)). These are not three independent confirmations: the MMWR authors note their R₀ "closely matched" their input assumption, making it **partly an artefact of the prior**, and both the phylodynamic and epiforecasts values import their generation interval wholesale from West African EBOV. The phylodynamic authors quantify the consequence — varying mean generation time from 12 to 18 days shifts the pre-outbreak R estimate from 1.71 to 2.16 — so any R used for Kenyan planning inherits that uncertainty ([Virological.org](https://virological.org/t/reproduction-number-of-the-2026-bundibugyo-virus-disease-outbreak-over-time-phylodynamic-estimates-from-genomes-collected-up-to-6-september-2026/1047)).

Against this, **length of hospital stay — the load-bearing parameter for bed-day modelling — essentially does not exist in the BVD literature.** The single most relevant source, the Isiro 2012 DRC cohort, reports *duration of illness from symptom onset* (median **18 days, range 8–26, in survivors; 15 days, range 4–28, in decedents; n = 9 per arm**), not hospital length of stay, which it does not report at all ([Kratz et al., PLOS ONE 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0129333)). The closest proxy for occupied bed-days in fatal cases is the epiforecasts admission-to-death Gamma (shape ~ Normal(2.151, 0.604), scale ~ Normal(3.906, 1.381)), fitted to the Isiro line list — but the matching survivor admission-to-recovery prior was not present in the retrievable methods text ([epiforecasts methods](https://epiforecasts.io/BVDOutbreakSize/stable/methods)). The counterintuitive implication for Kenya is that survivor stay exceeds fatal-case stay (18 vs 15 days by duration of illness), so a *falling* CFR *increases* mean bed occupancy per admission as more patients survive into prolonged convalescence. With n = 9 per arm, no stable distribution is supportable, and the strong recommendation from the notes is to carry LOS as an explicit scenario axis (e.g. 10/14/18 days for survivors, 8/12/15 for fatal cases) **labelled as scenario values, not citable estimates**, because sensitivity to LOS will dominate sensitivity to R.

The delay parameters that link surveillance to transmission are partially usable. **Incubation period** has a BDBV-derived mean of **11.3 days** from Isiro — but on only **3 patients** (values 2, 5, 27 days), which the authors caution may be unrepresentative — conflicting with the epiforecasts prior mean of **6.3 days**, a near-twofold discrepancy the notes could not resolve ([PLOS ONE 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0129333); [epiforecasts methods](https://epiforecasts.io/BVDOutbreakSize/stable/methods)). **Onset-to-admission** at Isiro was short (**61% admitted on days 1–3**), though the source's own percentages are internally inconsistent with its statement that 6 of 18 cases were admitted on day 10 or later ([PLOS ONE 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0129333)); the MMWR model simply assumed a **2-day delay to isolation** ([MMWR via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13257927/)). Two parameters are genuinely absent: the **serial/generation interval has no BDBV estimate whatsoever** and is universally substituted from West African EBOV (gamma, **mean 15.3 days, SD 9.3 days**), which the phylodynamic authors state explicitly ([Virological.org](https://virological.org/t/reproduction-number-of-the-2026-bundibugyo-virus-disease-outbreak-over-time-phylodynamic-estimates-from-genomes-collected-up-to-6-september-2026/1047)); and the **death-to-burial interval was not found in any retrieved source** beyond a single Kenyan observation of a 1-day interval ([WHO AFRO Kenya](https://afro.who.int/countries/kenya/news/kenya-confirms-first-imported-bundibugyo-virus-disease-case-who-supports-control-efforts)).

The case-fatality picture carries the single most planning-relevant number in the entire parameter set. The 2026 DRC crude **CFR was 48.2% as of 6 October (8,728 confirmed cases, 4,205 deaths)**, up from **30.9% on 1 July**, with North Kivu at **57.7%** ([WHO DON619, 8 Oct 2026](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2026-DON619); [WHO DON612, 3 Jul 2026](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2026-DON612)). Crucially, **of 372 deaths reported in one DON interval, 264 (≈71%) occurred in communities rather than treatment centres** ([WHO DON619](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2026-DON619)). This is the key design insight: if Kenya resembles DRC, most deaths would occur outside isolation beds, so **bed demand scales with surveillance performance (detected cases), not with incidence** — capacity sized on projected deaths would overestimate beds while understating the need for community case-finding, safe burial and transport. Health workers made up **~7% of DRC cases (102/1,460) as of 1 July 2026** ([WHO DON612](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2026-DON612)), and DRC contact follow-up ran persistently below target, at **80.4% on 4 October (23,741 of 29,535 contacts)** and oscillating between 74.7% and 87.6% within three weeks ([WHO DON619](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2026-DON619)).

Two gaps must be stated plainly in the article. **Overdispersion (negative-binomial k) could not be verified from any primary BDBV source** — neither 2026 BDBV model reports it, and the West African anchors (Althaus, Lau, Faye) were not retrievable in full; any k used will be an EBOV substitution ([MMWR via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13257927/); [epiforecasts BVDOutbreakSize](https://epiforecasts.io/BVDOutbreakSize/stable/)). And **two brief citations are wrong and must be corrected**: de Padua & Akhmetzhanov (*Emerg Infect Dis* 2026, doi:10.3201/eid3210.261175) is a **reanalysis of Uganda 2007, not the 2026 outbreak**, giving **R = 1.55 and CFR 31%→25%** as historical values ([authors' repository](https://github.com/aakhmetz/Bundibugyo2007-transmission)); and the MMWR paper is a **modelled scenario projection, not an observational estimate**. Treating either as a 2026-outbreak observation would misrepresent the evidence.

## Delayed feedback and scarce beds both bend the equilibrium through bifurcation

The article's two methodological pillars are each supported by a mature, directly transferable literature — and the decisive point is that **neither has been applied to Ebola**, which the notes identify as a genuine novelty claim. On the delay side, a well-developed body of work shows that latency in a feedback loop — incidence, media awareness, or control decisions responding to *past* prevalence — destabilises the endemic equilibrium through a **Hopf bifurcation at a critical delay τ₀**. The canonical derivation sets λ = iω in a characteristic equation of the form **P(λ) + Q(λ)e^{−λτ} = 0**, solves |P(iω)| = |Q(iω)| for ω₀, and obtains τ_n = (1/ω₀)·arccos(·) + 2nπ/ω₀, with direction and stability from Hassard-type normal-form or centre-manifold calculations. Sirijampa, Chinviriyasit & Chinviriyasit (2018) work this through for a delayed SEIR model, reducing the endemic characteristic equation to **λ³ + a₂λ² + a₁λ + a₀ + (b₂λ² + b₁λ + b₀)e^{−λτ} = 0** and finding the equilibrium stable on [0, τ₀) and losing stability at τ₀ ≈ 25.86 weeks for R₀ = 3.1 — importantly, a **subcritical** Hopf (μ₂ < 0) with unstable periodic orbits, meaning large perturbations can escape even below τ₀ ([*Adv Difference Equ* 2018:348](https://advancesincontinuousanddiscretemodels.springeropen.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s13662-018-1805-6.pdf)).

The conceptually closest match to a surveillance-to-response delay is the information-feedback family. d'Onofrio & Manfredi model an information index M(t) as a distributed-delay memory of past prevalence and find that **only intermediate delays destabilise** the equilibrium, and only with a sufficiently sharp (Erlang n ≥ 2) memory kernel: for one parameter set the unstable range runs from mean delays of ~55 days to ~10 years, and below a threshold information-sensitivity the equilibrium is stable for any delay ([arXiv:1309.3327](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3327)). Greenhalgh et al. (2015) give the most literal analogue, an awareness-campaign intensity driven by infections τ₂ time units earlier (dM/dt = μY(t−τ₂) − μ₀M), with a Hopf at **τ₂₀ ≈ 146 days** that falls to ~90 days when a second (memory-fading) delay is active, demonstrating that two delays interact destabilisingly ([Strathprints PDF](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/50882/1/Greenhalgh_AMC_2015_Awareness_programs_control_infectious_disease.pdf)). Media-delay models give Hopf points around h₀ ≈ 146.9 days ([Li et al., arXiv:1609.03925](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.03925)), and a 2025 SIHR model with multiple delays confirms Hopf at critical thresholds with hospital-bed availability as a control lever ([Ismail et al., *J Appl Math Comput* 2025](https://research.uaeu.ac.ae/en/publications/analysis-and-hopf-bifurcation-of-an-sihr-epidemic-model-with-mult/)). The honest caveat the notes stress: these critical delays (26 weeks, 90–146 days) are long relative to plausible Ebola surveillance delays of days to weeks, because they are endemic-equilibrium analyses on demographic timescales. For an acute Ebola epidemic a Hopf analysis is a **stylised device** showing whether delayed feedback induces overshoot and resurgence, not a literal limit-cycle prediction — and the first Lyapunov coefficient should be computed rather than a supercritical bifurcation assumed. Where the equilibrium itself depends on τ (e.g. a survival factor e^{−μτ}), the **Beretta–Kuang (2002) geometric stability-switch criterion** with its S_n(τ) functions should be used instead, though that method description comes from training knowledge because SIAM blocked retrieval and must be verified ([SIAM record](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/S0036141000376086)).

On the capacity side, the literature is equally clear and more directly usable. Since Wang & Ruan (2004) modelled a **constant removal rate** of infectives, limited treatment capacity has been known to produce backward bifurcation, bistability, saddle-node, Hopf and Bogdanov–Takens bifurcations, so **disease can persist when R₀ < 1** ([as summarised in Sun et al., arXiv q-bio/0610006](https://arxiv.org/pdf/q-bio/0610006); [MMNP 2021 reference list](https://www.mmnp-journal.org/articles/mmnp/ref/2021/01/mmnp200039/mmnp200039.html)). The canonical hospital-bed formulation is the Shan & Zhu school: a nonlinear recovery rate **μ(b,I) = μ₀ + (μ₁ − μ₀)·3b²/(I² + 3b²)** that depends on bed number b, yielding a forward transcritical bifurcation at R₀ = 1 but persistence for R₀ < 1 unless b exceeds a threshold b⁺(μ₁), with saddle-node, cusp, generic Hopf and nilpotent Bogdanov–Takens of codimension ≥ 3 ([Shan, Yi & Zhu, *J Differ Equ* 2016;260:4339–4365](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~yingfei/Shan2.pdf)); the related 2014 paper found Bogdanov–Takens of codimension 3 as beds vary ([Shan & Zhu, *J Differ Equ* 2014](https://huaiping.mathstats.yorku.ca/publication/bifurcations-and-complex-dynamics-of-an-sir-model-with-the-impact-of-the-number-of-hospital-beds)). Several papers derive an explicit **critical bed number b_c** above which elimination is possible ([Li & Zhang, *PLoS ONE* 2017](https://doaj.org/article/89906274a9a2403fbb8d41f2f43135b7)), and the saturation-recovery literature reaches the same policy conclusion — "a sufficient number of beds is critical" and treatment capacity can be weighed against cost ([Wan & Cui, *J Appl Math* 2013](https://emis.univie.ac.at/journals/HOA/JAM/Volume2013/314958.abs.html)).

The structurally closest analogue to a Bundibugyo isolation-bed model is Jia, Wang & Chen (2025), whose **hospitalisation rate is a nonlinear function of *unoccupied* beds**: as total beds fall, the system shifts from forward to backward and saddle-node bifurcation, and below a bed threshold the disease persists with R < 1, so the authors recommend raising beds above that threshold during outbreaks ([*Math Comput Simul* 2025;229:706–724](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/matcom/v229y2025icp706-724.html)). This suggests the natural Ebola construction: an isolation rate of the form ρ·(B − H)₊/B or ρ·B/(B + I), where B is ETU/isolation beds and isolated patients H are far less transmissible than community cases I, giving two bifurcation handles — the critical bed number b_c (backward bifurcation/bistability) and the Hopf points in b. The policy translation is sharp and matches the empirical Ebola record below: **backward bifurcation in b means that pushing effective R below 1 with other measures is insufficient when beds are scarce — once community prevalence exceeds bed capacity, the system can jump to a high-prevalence state.** The important limitation to flag is that **none of these bed-bifurcation papers is Ebola-specific**, and the Shan–Zhu and Li–Zhang exact recovery functions and b_c formulae come partly from training knowledge because PMC/ScienceDirect retrieval was blocked.

## Stochastic framing turns overdispersion and bed limits into outbreak probability and sizing

For an importation-driven process in Nairobi or Mombasa — small numbers, stochastic, with R locally set by IPC and contact tracing — a branching-process framing fits far better than the established-transmission R(t) trajectories from DRC. The foundational result is Lloyd-Smith et al. (2005): individual reproductive number ν ~ Gamma(mean R, shape k) gives a **negative-binomial(R, k) offspring distribution**, and the extinction probability q is the smallest root in [0,1] of **q = (k/(k + R(1 − q)))^k**, so that **P(extinct | n index cases) = qⁿ** by independence of lineages ([*Nature* 2005;438:355–359, via York CMDP](https://cmdp.lamps.yorku.ca/?p=976); [CRAN superspreading](https://cran.nics.utk.edu/cran/web/packages/superspreading/readme/README.html)). The critical fact for Ebola is **strong overdispersion**: Althaus (2015), reanalysing 152 cases from Conakry, found **R = 0.95 (95% CI 0.57–1.34) and k = 0.18 (0.10–0.26)** — similar to SARS — with **72% of cases generating no secondary case**, so most simulated outbreaks died out quickly while a few exceeded 100 cases ([Althaus PDF](https://boris.unibe.ch/68084/15/Althaus2015LancetInfDis.pdf)). Lau et al. (2017) found **61% of cases caused by 3% of infected people** in the Freetown area ([LSHTM news](https://www.lshtm.ac.uk/newsevents/news/2017/ebola_super_spreaders.html)). With k this small, q for a single introduction is high even when R > 1, so imported-case scenarios (n = 1, 3, 5) give strongly non-linear establishment probabilities that should be reported alongside the deterministic R₀ — and omitting k, as both 2026 BDBV models do, understates both early stochastic extinction and the explosive upper tail that capacity planning exists to cover.

The Ebola stochastic-modelling literature converges on one message: **isolation-bed capacity is a dominant, quantifiable determinant of outbreak size, and earlier scale-up averts disproportionately more cases.** Camacho et al. (2014), fitting the 1976 Yambuku outbreak and simulating stochastically with community, funeral and hospital routes, found **R₀ = 4.71 (3.92–5.66)** and that ~3% of 10,000 runs exceeded 1,000 cases, rising to ~35% without hospital transmission control ([*Epidemics* 2014, LSHTM PDF](https://lshtm-eprints-repo-prod.lshtm-eprints.cdl.cosector.com/2030883/2/1-s2.0-S1755436514000528-main.pdf)). The pivotal study for facility sizing is Kucharski et al. (2015), a stochastic district-level model fitted with particle MCMC in which **ETU admission depended on bed availability** — admitted with probability 1 if a bed was free, 0 otherwise, leaving the patient infectious in the community — which is the exact stochastic analogue of the empty-bed-dependent hospitalisation rate in Jia et al. (2025). The **2,971 added beds averted 56,600 cases (95% CI 48,300–84,500); introducing them four weeks earlier would have averted 12,500 more** ([PNAS 2015;112(46):14366–71, PMC4655521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4655521/)). Lewnard et al. (2014) in Montserrado found that **4,800 beds plus five-fold ascertainment deployed in November averted 77,312 cases, but the same package by 15 October would have averted 137,432**, describing a "rapidly closing window of opportunity" ([MIDAS](https://midasnetwork.us/papers/dynamics-and-control-of-ebola-virus-transmission-in-montserrado-liberia-a-mathematical-modelling-analysis/)). The Bundibugyo-specific anchor is the MMWR branching-process model: calibrated to 50 deaths with a 2-day onset-to-isolation delay, **at 20% isolation 65% of runs reached ≥20,000 cases, but at 70% isolation 94% of runs stayed below 10,000** ([MMWR via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13257927/)).

For the simulation engine itself, the standard algorithms are Gillespie's exact direct method (1976, 1977) and tau-leaping (2001), with the leap condition **|Δa_j(x)| ≤ ε·a_j(x), ε ≈ 0.03–0.05** for step-size selection ([Cao, Gillespie & Petzold adaptive tau-leaping](https://cse.cs.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/publications/adaptivetau06.pdf)). The deterministic foundations are equally standard: the next-generation matrix **K = FV⁻¹ with R₀ = ρ(FV⁻¹)** (van den Driessche & Watmough 2002, reference confirmed but page not fetched), the operator definition of R₀ ([Diekmann, Heesterbeek & Metz, *J Math Biol* 1990;28:365–382](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF00178324)), and the Euler–Lotka link between growth rate r and R₀, R₀ = 1/M(−r) ([Wallinga & Lipsitch, *Proc R Soc B* 2007](https://cmdp.lamps.yorku.ca/?p=976)). For the planned two-city structure, **separable (proportionate) mixing β_ij = a_i b_j makes K rank one**, so R₀ reduces to its trace — the classic Diekmann–Heesterbeek result. The important honesty note: the next-generation, final-size and Euler–Lotka formulas come from training knowledge because the books and several PMC pages could not be fetched, and must be verified against the texts before quoting. Equally, **no published paper was found that uses peak-occupancy quantiles from stochastic simulations to size Ebola isolation capacity** — quantile-based sizing is common in COVID-19 ICU planning but must be presented as the authors' own design choice, taking (say) the 90th–95th percentile of peak occupancy from Gillespie/tau-leaping runs as the bed target.

## Isolation, burial, tracing and speed each have measured, transferable effect sizes

The intervention literature gives the article its empirical calibration, with the essential caveat that **every effect size comes from Zaire ebolavirus or Sudan virus, never Bundibugyo** — transferring them is an assumption the article must state. The dominant lever is the fraction of cases effectively isolated. Meltzer et al. (2014) quantified transmission per infectious person by setting — **0.12 secondary cases in an ETU, 0.18 in community isolation with safe burial, and 1.8 at home without isolation** — and concluded that **~70% of patients in ETUs or effective community isolation ends the epidemic**, while each **30-day delay in scale-up roughly tripled peak daily cases** (1,335 → 4,178 → 10,184) ([MMWR Suppl 2014;63(3):1–14](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/su6303a1.htm)). The per-setting values give a clean way to convert "fraction isolated" into effective R, and with R₀ near 1.8–2.5 they imply a control threshold of isolating ~50–70% of cases — consistent with the WHO Ebola Response Team's judgment that transmission needed to fall by "a little more than half" ([NEJM 2014;371:1481–95, PDF mirror](https://m.chinacdc.cn/jkzt/crb/qt/ablcxr/gwjszl/201411/W020141105625879288408.pdf)). Washington & Meltzer (2015) showed the effect is synergistic: **ETUs alone averted 2,244 cases, community care centres alone 4,487, both together 9,097**, with each percentage-point increase in isolation averting 112–165 cases ([PMC4584561](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4584561)). This supports modelling "effective isolation slots" — a tiered mix of ETU beds plus decentralised holding/community units — rather than ETU beds alone, and Kucharski's assumption that **50% of holding-centre beds were occupied by Ebola-negative patients** argues for sizing suspect/holding isolation at roughly **2× the confirmed-case requirement** in a high-background-febrile-illness setting like malaria-endemic Mombasa ([PMC4655521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4655521/)).

Safe and dignified burial has a direct, quantified effect. Tiffany et al. (2017), investigating 45 unsafe burials, estimated **a mean 2.58 secondary cases per unsafe burial** and that the Red Cross SDB programme **potentially averted 1,411–10,452 cases (a 4.9–36.5% smaller epidemic)**, with caregivers who had contact before death **2.63–5.97× more likely to be infected** than those with post-mortem contact only ([PMC5480832](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5480832)). Funerals accounted for **8.6% of infections in Liberia** ([Merler et al., *Lancet Infect Dis* 2015, CIDID](https://www.cidid.org/publications-1/2015/1/29/spatiotemporal-spread-of-the-2014-outbreak-of-ebola-virus-disease-in-liberia-and-the-effectiveness-of-non-pharmaceutical-interventions-a-computational-modelling-analysis)) and **15% early in Conakry, falling to 4% after controls** when funeral R dropped below 0.1 ([Faye et al., Institut Pasteur record](https://research.pasteur.fr/en/b/6Gj)). A defensible scenario is therefore a 5–15% funeral share absent SDB, largely removable with safe burial — and because community deaths need SDB teams rather than beds, this is a distinct resource line in the capacity model.

Contact tracing and speed-to-isolation complete the intervention set. West African tracing was weak — in Liberia it was **started for only 26.7% of cases and detected 3.6% of new cases**, though **88% of enrolled contacts completed monitoring** ([Swanson et al., PMC6152989](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6152989)) — whereas DRC 2019 reached **74% of new cases arising from registered contacts** ([WHO DON, Dec 2019](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2019-DON225)). The theoretical target is the **critical traced fraction (ρφ)_c = 1 − 1/R₀**, about 45–60% for R₀ of 1.8–2.5, with R_e = R₀(1 − ρφ) under perfect monitoring; Browne et al. found **coverage mattered more than speed**, and that hospitalising at 3 rather than 5 days lowered simulated R_e from ~1.01 to ~0.87 at 75% reporting ([arXiv:1505.03821](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.03821)). Because being a known contact mainly shortens the onset-to-isolation delay, tracing is best modelled *through* the delay distribution rather than as a separate R multiplier. On delay itself, West African onset-to-hospitalisation averaged **5.0 ± 4.7 days against onset-to-death of 7.5 days**, so most infectiousness occurred in the community; Sierra Leone cut onset-to-attendance from **4.6 to 1.3 days**, and R was negatively correlated with the proportion hospitalised within ≤4 days (**r = −0.37, p < 0.001**) ([NEJM 2014](https://m.chinacdc.cn/jkzt/crb/qt/ablcxr/gwjszl/201411/W020141105625879288408.pdf); [WHO ERT, *PLoS Med* 2016, RKI edoc](https://edoc.rki.de/handle/176904/2476?show=full); [PMC4655521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4655521/)). The Fraser et al. (2004) framework — an outbreak is controllable by isolation alone when pre-symptomatic transmission θ is small relative to 1/R₀ — underpins the claim that Ebola is isolation-controllable if isolation is fast, though its full text was blocked and θ for Ebola was not verified ([PMC395937](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/395937/)). These values anchor surveillance-delay scenarios of (say) 5, 3 and 1.5 days for Nairobi and Mombasa.

Finally, **nosocomial amplification is consistently large early in outbreaks** and is the dominant risk pathway for an importation-driven Kenyan epidemic. Health-care-associated transmission was **26% of cases in Uganda 2022** (with 60% of early patients first seeking care at private facilities), **16.6–19.1% in DRC 2018–20** (69.5% of implicated facilities with ownership data were private), and **~38% in Liberia** ([Ario et al., *Lancet Glob Health* 2024, UNCST repository](https://nru.uncst.go.ug/items/fa25363e-3b90-48d3-897a-d1184e1d4dcb/full); [Baller et al., PMC8755545](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8755545); [Merler et al., CIDID](https://www.cidid.org/publications-1/2015/1/29/spatiotemporal-spread-of-the-2014-outbreak-of-ebola-virus-disease-in-liberia-and-the-effectiveness-of-non-pharmaceutical-interventions-a-computational-modelling-analysis)). Health-worker case shares ran **5% (DRC cumulative) to 13.4% (Uganda SVD)** ([WHO DON Dec 2019](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2019-DON225); [Wailagala et al., NEJM 2024, UNCST](https://nru.uncst.go.ug/items/4ce3939e-67a7-4869-a339-0c525f4b2601/full)). For Kenyan cities with large private and informal outpatient sectors, a **pre-detection nosocomial share of ~15–35%** is a defensible scenario range, and because HCW losses also reduce staffed bed capacity, isolation beds and IPC quality cannot be treated as separable inputs. Several intervention DOIs (Ario 2024; Wailagala 2024; Merler 2015; Faye 2015) were constructed from PIIs or memory and need verification.

## Kenya's 2026 importation exposes a Nairobi-heavy, Mombasa-thin bed geography

Kenya's own outbreak is small but structurally revealing, and it defines the supply side the model must capture. The index case was a Kenyan man who fell ill in the DRC, travelled by road to Kampala and **flew into Nairobi on 3 October 2026**, was taken by a relative to The Nairobi Hospital and isolated, **tested positive on 5 October at both NVRL and KEMRI, and died the same night**, with a safe and dignified burial on 6 October ([WHO DON619](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2026-DON619); [WHO AFRO Kenya](https://afro.who.int/countries/kenya/news/kenya-confirms-first-imported-bundibugyo-virus-disease-case-who-supports-control-efforts)). A **second case — a 39-year-old relative already in quarantine — developed symptoms on 13 October and was confirmed on 14 October**, which MoH framed as "the response working as designed," since contact tracing caught it ([AP/ABC, 14 Oct 2026](https://abcnews.com/International/wireStory/kenya-confirms-ebola-case-relative-patient-137261113); [The Standard](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/health-science/article/2001560133/kenya-confirms-second-ebola-case-as-contact-tracing-intensifies)). Contacts grew from 57 (7 Oct) to **128 identified with 108 quarantined by mid-October** ([Citizen Digital, 11 Oct 2026](https://citizen.digital/article/ebola-122-contacts-verified-108-in-quarantine-as-tracing-continues-n391840); [The Standard](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/health-science/article/2001560133/kenya-confirms-second-ebola-case-as-contact-tracing-intensifies)). Kenya's crude CFR of 50% rests on 1 death in 2 cases and carries no information. The telling detail for the model's importation module: the index case **passed through JKIA screening undetected** because he was taken to hospital privately, had a month-long pre-diagnosis illness, a multi-country journey, and private-hospital presentation — the classic failure points for importation detection.

The institutional context is that **the Kenya National Public Health Institute (KNPHI), legally created in 2022 but only operational from 8 May 2025, is facing its first major test.** It absorbed 13 MoH divisions including surveillance, port health and the PHEOC, and **activated the national Ebola Incident Management System on 20 May 2026** ([IANPHI, Aug 2025](https://ianphi.org/news/2025/kenya-launches-its-nphi.html); [Citizen Digital, 25 Jun 2026](https://www.citizen.digital/article/over-140k-travellers-screened-for-ebola-no-positive-case-recorded-cs-duale-n385247)). By 11 October 2026, Kenya had **screened 667,675 travellers at 26 points of entry, tested 293 samples, trained 4,917 health workers, and held PCR confirmation to 6–12 hours after referral from any county** ([Citizen Digital, 11 Oct 2026](https://citizen.digital/article/ebola-122-contacts-verified-108-in-quarantine-as-tracing-continues-n391840)). But the best Kenya-specific surveillance-timeliness evidence is sobering: a 2026 medRxiv preprint on 28 outbreaks in four counties (2021–2025) found **only 53.6% met all three 7-1-7 targets**, with the 7-day *response* target the weakest link (68.8% for event-based surveillance) ([Mamuti, Ngere et al., medRxiv, Sciety](https://sciety.org/articles/activity/10.64898/2026.08.06.26359848)). This directly justifies a delayed or incomplete-response scenario in the model. No KNPHI-branded sitrep or dashboard was found, and every designated Ebola lab sits in Nairobi or western Kenya, **none on the Coast** — so Mombasa samples face a long referral journey that the national 6–12-hour figure (time after receipt) does not capture.

The decisive input for a Nairobi-vs-Mombasa comparison is the **profound bed asymmetry**. The 7 October MoH breakdown gives **KNH 8, MTRH 50, National Police Hospital 49, Port Reitz (Mombasa) 4, and The Nairobi Hospital 130 — summing to ~241 beds** ([Citizen Digital, 7 Oct 2026](https://citizen.digital/article/five-isolation-centres-designated-as-govt-steps-up-ebola-preparedness-n391619)). This means **~54% of designated isolation beds sit in one private hospital, Nairobi holds ~78% of all beds, and Mombasa has just 4 designated beds (~1.7%)** plus Utange as an unquantified site. Mombasa is a fully urban county of **1.21 million people at 5,495/km² with only 33 beds and 19 health workers per 10,000 (2019)**, served by Coast General and a 4-bed MSF-supported Port Reitz unit ([CRA County Fact Sheet](https://ipfglobal.or.ke/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Mombasa-County-Kenya-County-Fact-Sheets-3rd-Edition-by-CRA.pdf); [MSF, 15 Jul 2026](https://msf.or.ke/news-and-resources/news/mombasa-county-strengthens-ebola-disease-preparedness-new-training-facility)). At roughly **one isolation bed per 300,000 residents**, Mombasa would need surge capacity or Nairobi transfers almost immediately in any cluster — a quantitative statement of exactly the bed-scarcity regime where the backward-bifurcation theory predicts the system can jump to a high-prevalence state. The port compounds the exposure: Mombasa handled **2.11 m TEU and 15.88 Mt of transit cargo in 2025, with DRC-bound transit up 16.5%** ([Africa Sustainability Matters, citing KPA](https://africasustainabilitymatters.com/kenyas-port-transit-cargo-grows-19-5-in-2025-as-mombasa-strengthens-regional-trade-role/)). Nairobi's exposure runs through JKIA, which handled **~8.6 million passengers in 2025 against a 7.5 m design capacity** and is the mandatory entry point for travellers from the 10 monitored countries ([The Star, 2 Jan 2026](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/2026-01-02-kaa-modern-jkia-airport-critical-as-passengers-exceed-capacity)). Slum populations — a key denominator — are highly uncertain, with Kibera cited at anywhere from **170,070 (2019 census) to 500,000–1 million** ([People Daily, Mar 2020](https://peopledaily.digital/news/use-slum-population-figures-to-improve-livelihoods)), so the model should carry them as sensitivity ranges. On burial, the Kenya Red Cross is MoH-designated for emergency burials, and the 2020 COVID-19 precedent shows Coast Muslim leaders adapted rites (no ritual washing, body bags, burial within 24–48 hours), which is compatible with safe burial if a team is present but risky for deaths occurring at home before notification ([The Standard, Oct 2026](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/health-science/article/2001560007/ebola-why-safe-burial-protocols-matter-in-containing-virus); [Kenyans.co.ke, 13 Apr 2020](https://www.kenyans.co.ke/news/51990-muslims-warned-against-culture-washing-corpses)).

## Conclusion

The literature positions this article to make three claims that are both well-grounded and genuinely new. First, the **modelling novelty is real**: delayed-feedback Hopf analysis and limited-bed backward-bifurcation analysis are each mature, but neither has been applied to Ebola, and combining them around an empty-bed-dependent isolation rate (Jia-style) with a surveillance-to-response delay is unoccupied ground. Second, the **evidence is inverted relative to need** — transmissibility is triangulated to R ≈ 1.95–2.51 while length of stay, the parameter that actually sets bed-days, has no BDBV distribution — so the article's credibility depends on carrying LOS, overdispersion k, and slum denominators as explicit scenario axes rather than borrowed point estimates, and on stating that all intervention effect sizes are Zaire/Sudan-virus substitutions. Third, Kenya's real geography makes the theory bite: with ~78% of isolation beds in Nairobi and only 4 in Mombasa, and with ~71% of DRC deaths occurring in communities rather than beds, the model's most policy-relevant output is not a single national bed number but the **interaction between surveillance performance, response delay, and the critical bed threshold b_c in each city** — the point at which scarce beds flip the system from controllable to bistable.

The sharpest forward implication is methodological humility paired with design clarity. The two sources carrying the most parameter detail (the Virological.org phylodynamics and the epiforecasts methods) are **not peer-reviewed**, the phylodynamic authors call their own August decline "not established," and a substantial number of foundational formulas and DOIs in these notes come from training knowledge because CDC, SIAM, PMC and ScienceDirect blocked automated retrieval — every such instance is flagged above and must be verified against the primary source before publication. The highest-value verification targets are the EID Uganda-2007 appendix credible intervals, the MMWR supplementary delay-distribution box, the Sierra Leone ETU length-of-stay study (PMC8274045), and a BDBV-plausible k; closing even the last of these would materially strengthen the stochastic outbreak-probability results that are, for an importation-driven two-city setting, the part of the model that matters most.
